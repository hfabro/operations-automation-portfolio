QR-Based Digital Ladder Inspection

A recurring safety inspection was structured around unique asset identity, a mobile checklist, controlled responses, durable history, notifications, and follow-up for failed conditions.

Operational problem

Inspect the right asset. Preserve the right history.

Recurring ladder inspections needed a dependable way to identify the exact asset, capture consistent inspection responses, track completion, retain evidence, and surface failures that require follow-up.

Constraint

Asset-level accountability

A generic form is not enough. Every record needs a durable link to one ladder, its location and status, the specific occurrence, and the evidence produced by that inspection.

Approach

QR entry into a controlled workflow

The QR route identifies the asset before the inspector completes structured pass/fail criteria, notes, status, and any remove-from-service decision.

IdentityUnique ladder asset and QR route
ExecutionMobile checklist with controlled criteria
ControlFailure handling and follow-up state
Accurate delivery statement

A functional version of the inspection application was taken from initial design through working implementation in approximately one day using rapid requirements definition, iterative development, testing, and AI-assisted troubleshooting. This describes rapid functional delivery—not completion of an enterprise platform or the full organizational rollout around it.

Architecture

Identity before interface.

The asset record anchors the process. The inspection occurrence remains separate so recurring work can preserve a complete history without overwriting the asset or a prior inspection.

SharePoint / ListsAsset, occurrence, response, and status records

History & reportingCompletion, failure, follow-up, management visibility
  1. Asset identity prevents ambiguity.
    The inspection must refer to a stable record, not whatever description the user happens to type.
  2. Occurrences preserve recurrence.
    Each scheduled inspection becomes its own record, protecting history and making missed work visible.
  3. Responses remain structured.
    Controlled criteria make failures reportable while notes retain necessary context.

Controls & failure modes

Design for the failed inspection.

Execution controls

Reliable input

  • Asset must resolve before inspection
  • Required criteria cannot be silently skipped
  • Pass/fail responses remain structured
  • Notes and evidence add context without replacing criteria
  • Completion status is explicit
Exception controls

Visible follow-up

  • Failed items become exceptions, not buried comments
  • Remove-from-service decisions are prominent
  • Notifications route attention to owners
  • Follow-up state remains linked to the source inspection
  • History supports later review and reporting

Outcome

A working inspection system, not a digital form.

The result demonstrates how a narrow operational problem can move quickly from requirements to a functional application while still respecting data structure, recurrence, exception handling, history, and reporting readiness.

User experienceScan, verify, inspect, submit
Operational visibilityCompletion and failure status by asset
Management useHistory, exceptions, and reporting-ready records

What I learned

Speed is useful only when structure survives it.

  1. Rapid requirements definition works best when the asset, occurrence, response, and exception entities are named early.
  2. AI-assisted troubleshooting accelerates iteration, but validation and deterministic business rules still govern the result.
  3. A convincing prototype should expose the operational lifecycle, not simply prove that a screen can save a record.
